# Interview Preparation — 10 Questions & Answers

### 1. Explain your project.
I built the Social Media Privacy Risk Assessment Framework: a defensive cybersecurity and privacy
tool that measures how exposed someone's social-media presence is, without ever scraping or
tracking anyone. The user answers 58 self-reported questions across ten categories — profile
visibility, personal information, location, posts and content, connections, tagging, account
security, third-party apps, social engineering and digital footprint. The backend is FastAPI; it
validates the input, converts answers into numeric features in `extract_privacy_features()`,
computes ten category scores from 0 to 100, applies a configurable weighting model — personal
information, location and account security carry 15% each — and produces an overall 0–100 score
where higher means more exposure, mapped to LOW, MODERATE, HIGH or CRITICAL. From the same
features a findings engine raises severity-rated findings, each with an explanation and a
recommended action, and a recommendation engine prioritises them as IMMEDIATE, IMPORTANT or GOOD
PRACTICE. There's also an improvement simulator: you tick changes like "enable MFA" or "make phone
private" and it re-scores the same questionnaire — my synthetic demo profile goes from 84.48
CRITICAL to 28.11 MODERATE. On top of that I implemented a second, complementary model from the
project rubric: an eight-dimension exposure score — PII 25 points, geolocation and EXIF 20,
children or minors in media 15, workplace and school disclosures 10, weak privacy settings 10,
linkage risks 10, handle correlation 5 and third-party trackers 5 — where the final score is
min(100, sum of capped contributions). That model can also analyse a voluntarily supplied
synthetic profile.json and posts.json export through a rule engine, and I was careful to document
that the two models are complementary rather than mathematically equivalent. Everything is visualised in a dashboard with a radar chart and five other
charts, and exported as an HTML or PDF report. The part I'm proudest of is the privacy
engineering: the app never asks for a real phone number or birth date, only whether that
information is publicly visible, and the database stores nothing but assessment id, scores,
finding types and timestamps. I proved that with automated tests that scan the raw SQLite file and
every generated report for phone, e-mail, date-of-birth and password patterns. 76 tests run and pass, and the
test-result matrix is generated by the test run itself.

### 2. What is the difference between privacy and security, and how does your project handle both?
Privacy is about who can see what about you; security is about who can get into your account.
Public birth date is a privacy problem; a missing second factor is a security problem. They
interact — public information makes social engineering credible, and weak account controls turn
that into an incident. In my scoring model six categories are essentially privacy-side (profile,
personal information, location, content, tagging, digital footprint) and four are security-side or
mixed (connections, account security, third-party apps, social engineering). Account security
carries 15% of the total weight precisely because it's the control that turns exposure into
compromise.

### 3. What is a digital footprint and why did you make it its own category?
A digital footprint is everything about you that persists online — current posts, but also
search-engine indexing, abandoned accounts, re-used handles and years of old content. I made it a
separate category because you can have a perfectly configured current account and still be highly
exposed through an old public profile you forgot about. The four questions there ask whether
you've ever self-searched, whether you keep abandoned public accounts, how often you review
privacy settings, and whether you re-use the same handle everywhere — handle re-use is what links
separate accounts into one correlated identity.

### 4. How does oversharing increase social-engineering risk?
Social engineering borrows context. A message that correctly names your employer, your college or
the trip you're currently on feels pre-verified, and that legitimacy is what defeats caution —
nothing technical is bypassed. The dangerous part is aggregation: employer plus college plus
travel plus family names together produce a coherent picture. My awareness module explains this
conceptually and deliberately contains no message templates or pretext scripts, because the
project is strictly defensive. The countermeasures I teach are reduce the public raw material,
verify unusual requests out-of-band, slow down urgency, and never share a verification code.

### 5. Walk me through your privacy-risk scoring model.
Every answer option has a documented risk value from 0 to 1 — for visibility questions PUBLIC is
1.0, NOT SURE is 0.7, FRIENDS is 0.4 and PRIVATE is 0. Each question also has a weight within its
category; MFA has weight 2.0 because it's the single most consequential control. A category score
is the weighted average of its question risks times 100, so it's always 0–100. The overall score
is the sum of category scores times category weights, which always renormalise to 1.0, so a
partial override can't silently break the model. Thresholds are 20, 40 and 70. It's completely
deterministic: the API returns a `score_breakdown` showing each category's weight and contribution
and a `top_contributors` list of the ten highest-impact answers, so no number is unexplained. I
deliberately avoided a machine-learning model — with no ground-truth labels it would have been an
unexplainable black box, and for an awareness tool explainability matters more than sophistication.

### 6. How did you apply data minimisation?
Three layers. First, collection: the questionnaire only asks about visibility, never values, and
the validator has an explicit forbidden-field list — if a payload contains `phone`, `email`,
`dob`, `password` and so on, it's rejected with a 422. Second, storage: the schema has four tables
— assessments, category_scores, findings, recommendations — and there is literally no column for
personal data; raw answers are never persisted, they exist only for the duration of one request.
Third, verification: test SEC-09 saves assessments and then scans the raw database file with regex
detectors for phone, e-mail, date-of-birth, coordinate and password patterns, and SEC-10 checks
that no per-question answer columns exist. I also added `DELETE /api/assessment/{id}` for user
control, even though there is nothing identifying to delete. The clearest example is the
children/minors dimension: it is worth 15 of the 100 rubric points, but all three questions ask
only whether child-related media, posts or school information are *publicly visible* — I never
ask for a child's name, school or address, `child_name` is on the forbidden-field list, and the
optional OpenCV helper only counts faces with no recognition and no age inference. Tests SEC-18
and SEC-19 fail the build if anyone changes that.

### 7. Why is MFA weighted so heavily in your model?
Because it changes the outcome of the most common failure mode. Password re-use is widespread, and
unrelated site breaches leak credentials constantly; without a second factor a leaked password is
full account access. With MFA, the same leak is usually a non-event. So MFA is the highest-weighted
single question in the framework, its finding severity is CRITICAL and its recommendation priority
is IMMEDIATE. In the simulator, enabling MFA alone produces one of the largest single-change
reductions — which is exactly the lesson I want a user to take away.

### 8. What is the risk from third-party applications, and how do you assess it?
Connected apps keep API access long after you stop using them, which violates least privilege and
expands the attack surface — if that app's vendor is breached, your account data goes with it. I
score four things: how often connected apps are reviewed, whether social login is used widely,
whether unused integrations were removed, and whether permission scopes are checked before
authorising. Never reviewing apps raises a HIGH-severity finding with the recommendation to review
and revoke unnecessary access. It's 5% of the total weight — real, but smaller than account
security because the impact depends on what scopes were granted.

### 9. How is Privacy by Design actually implemented, not just documented?
I mapped all eight principles to code. Data minimisation is the schema and the forbidden-field
validator. Purpose limitation: stored data feeds only your own report and anonymised aggregates.
Least privilege: aggregates are read-only and admin actions need an `ADMIN_API_KEY` from the
environment. Privacy by default: no accounts, no cookies, no trackers, and Chart.js is vendored
locally so there isn't even a CDN request. Transparency: weights, thresholds and per-question
contributions are returned by the API and the disclaimer appears in the UI, the API and the
report. User control: the delete endpoint. Retention limitation: nothing identifying is retained.
Secure processing: validation, HTML escaping, security headers, rate limiting and a
sensitive-pattern guard that runs before any report is written to disk.

### 10. How did you test the project?
Two suites, 51 tests, all executed. The functional suite covers all the required scenarios —
fully private and fully public profiles, each individual risk driver such as public phone, public
birthday, real-time check-ins, MFA disabled, password re-use, apps not reviewed — plus category and
overall score calculation, the 20/40/70 boundaries, recommendation generation, the improvement
simulation, database save, report generation, and the whole second model: the eight rubric
categories, cap enforcement, children/minors, trackers, linkage risk, handle correlation, the
file-driven rule engine, plus API validation, API error handling and deletion. I also added determinism, weight-configurability
and minimum-coverage tests. The security suite covers headers, input validation, sensitive-field
rejection, XSS reflection, rate limiting, identifier validation, the authorisation key, secrets
handling, deletion and the data-minimisation scans. A pytest fixture records Test ID, scenario,
input, expected result, actual result and pass/fail for every test and writes
`docs/TEST_RESULTS.md` at the end of the session — so the evidence is generated by the run, not
typed by me. Two tests genuinely failed the first time: my phone-number regex was matching ISO
timestamps in the database, and the string "password_reuse" was matching as a password. I fixed
the detectors rather than weakening the checks, and documented that in the results.
