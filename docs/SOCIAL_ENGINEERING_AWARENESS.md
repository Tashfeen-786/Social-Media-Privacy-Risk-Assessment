# How Oversharing Can Increase Social-Engineering Risk

*Educational, conceptual and defensive. This document deliberately contains **no** message
templates, no pretext scripts and no personalised attack examples.*

## The core idea

Social engineering works by borrowing **context**. A message that references something true about
you feels legitimate, and legitimacy short-circuits caution. Nothing technical is bypassed — the
human verification step is. Every publicly visible detail is one more piece of context that a
stranger can borrow without ever contacting you first.

## Information categories that most often supply that context

| Category | What it typically enables (conceptually) | Defensive response |
|---|---|---|
| **Employer** | Work-themed pretexts: invoices, HR notices, IT "support" | Limit workplace visibility; verify internal requests through internal channels |
| **College / university** | Alumni, scholarship and placement themes; also common security-question answers | Restrict education fields; never use them as security answers |
| **Travel plans** | Timing: messages that arrive exactly while you are away and distracted | Post after returning; keep travel content to a limited audience |
| **Family references** | Emergency and relative-in-trouble themes that create urgency | Hide family links; agree a family verification phrase |
| **Interests / hobbies** | Affinity and giveaway themes matched to your enthusiasm | Keep interest lists non-public; treat unsolicited offers as suspicious |
| **Public events** | Proof of attendance makes follow-up contact plausible | Avoid live event check-ins; review tagged event photos |

## Why the combination matters more than any single field

One public field is usually harmless. The risk is **aggregation**: employer + college + travel +
family links together produce a coherent picture that makes an unsolicited approach feel
pre-verified. This is exactly why the framework scores ten categories rather than one, and why
the Digital Footprint category exists.

## Universal defensive rules

1. **Verify out-of-band.** Unusual requests from a "known" contact get confirmed by phone or in person.
2. **Never share verification codes.** No legitimate support process ever needs your OTP.
3. **Slow down urgency.** Manufactured time pressure is the most reliable warning sign.
4. **Navigate manually.** Type the address yourself instead of following an unexpected link.
5. **Reduce the raw material.** Less public context = fewer believable approaches.
6. **Report and warn.** Reporting cloned profiles protects your whole contact list.

## Classroom exercise (safe)

Ask learners to list, from their own *public* profile only, what a complete stranger could learn
in five minutes: employer, city, school, travel pattern, family names, routine. Then map each item
to the checklist action that removes it. No real target is ever researched and no message is ever
drafted.
